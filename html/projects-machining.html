Manual Machining | Los Angeles, CA (2023-Present)

I picked up manual machining in 2023 while working on Flagship Combat Robotics, where I was manufacturing chassis components on manual and CNC mills. I found running a lathe strangely meditative, and it turned into its own hobby outside of the team's build season.

Most of what I make isn't tied to a specific project. I'll take whatever stock is left over from a robot build and turn it into random shapes, just to practice a new technique or clean up my feel for the machine.

A manual lathe removes material by holding a rotating workpiece against a stationary cutting tool, letting you shape symmetric parts like shafts, bushings, and knobs by hand rather than through programmed toolpaths.

Because everything is controlled by hand — feed rate, depth of cut, tool position — it forces you to pay close attention to the material and the machine's behavior in a way CNC work doesn't. That's part of what makes it feel meditative to me: the fabrication process itself is the point, not just the resulting part.

It's also a useful complement to the CNC and 3D printing skills I built on Flagship Combat Robotics. Some parts are still faster and more satisfying to make by hand.
