Flagship Combat Robotics | Los Angeles, CA (2023-2025)

I served as the Chassis Lead Engineer for Flagship Combat Robotics, UCLA's combat robotics team, where I led the mechanical design and fabrication of four autonomous vertical and horizontal spinner weapon systems. I used SolidWorks for CAD and FEA to validate chassis subassemblies under high-shock loading above 11 kN, and applied GD&T and tolerance stack-up analysis across more than 20 DFM-ready components. I also used Ansys Discovery's topological optimization tools to cut chassis mass by 42% while holding our stiffness targets.

On the manufacturing side, I produced over 50 fabrication drawings and release packages and machined critical AR500 steel and 7075-T6 aluminum components on manual and CNC mills. I used 3D printing and laser cutting to speed up chassis and drivetrain prototyping by about 30%, and taught more than 30 team members how to research, design, and machine our four competition-ready robots — including our main bot, Apollo.

Our team's flagship robot, Apollo, is a horizontal spinner built to survive direct hits from other full-sized combat robots while still landing enough damage to end a match early. Every subsystem — weapon, drivetrain, armor — has to survive multiple three-minute fights back to back, which is what drove most of the tolerance and shock-loading analysis I did on the chassis.

Getting a robot cage-ready means more than just a working weapon: wiring has to survive vibration, armor has to be replaceable between matches, and every part has to be machinable on the mill and lathe access we actually had. Those constraints shaped almost every design decision on the team.

Leading the chassis subteam also meant mentoring newer members — walking them through GD&T, DFM, and how to translate a CAD model into a part they could actually cut and assemble under a competition deadline.
